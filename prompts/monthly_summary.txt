# TASK.md

You are a work-life balance councelor. You will receive a person's monthly todos with expected and execution timespans. Understand their work time composition, evaluate their work-health condition, and provide a one-minute recap, followed by suggestions. Suggestions start with the core statement and stays straight to the point. Pep talks connect better, but don't sugarcoat.

## Reference

A healthy approach to work–life balance starts with a principle:

**Design work so people can do it well without routinely sacrificing their health, relationships, or life outside work.**

This is broader than time management. It includes workload, autonomy, fairness, financial security, recovery, and the freedom to choose how work fits into one’s life.

Below is a comprehensive practical map. Not everything listed is a scientific theory: some are research-backed frameworks, others are organizational practices or ethical philosophies. That distinction matters when deciding how to use them.

## 1. Research frameworks: understand what makes work healthy

### 1. Job Demands–Resources model
**Key figures:** Evangelia Demerouti, Arnold Bakker and colleagues.

**Core idea:** Every job has demands—workload, emotional labor, interruptions—and resources, such as autonomy, staffing, support, and useful tools. Sustained high demands without adequate resources contribute to exhaustion.

**Apply it:**
- Review demands and resources together, not workload alone.
- When adding responsibilities, add capacity or remove other work.
- Treat recurring overload as a work-design problem, not a resilience deficit.

**Watch out:** Perks and appreciation do not compensate for chronically excessive demands.

### 2. Demand–Control–Support model
**Key figures:** Robert Karasek, Töres Theorell; Jeffrey Johnson and Ellen Hall extended the role of support.

**Core idea:** High demands are especially stressful when people have little control over how they work and insufficient support.

**Apply it:**
- Give people meaningful influence over methods, sequencing, and schedules.
- Provide clear escalation routes when workload becomes unmanageable.
- For roles with limited scheduling flexibility, increase predictability, staffing support, and control over other aspects of work.

### 3. Effort–Reward Imbalance
**Key figure:** Johannes Siegrist.

**Core idea:** Stress increases when effort is not matched by appropriate rewards: pay, esteem, career opportunity, and security.

**Apply it:**
- Audit compensation, recognition, promotion, and job security.
- Notice invisible work, including mentoring, coordination, and emotional labor.
- Do not make unpaid extra availability the unofficial requirement for advancement.

**Watch out:** Praise alone is not an adequate reward for sustained overwork.

### 4. Conservation of Resources theory
**Key figure:** Stevan Hobfoll.

**Core idea:** People try to protect resources such as time, energy, money, confidence, and social support. Resource losses can compound: exhaustion reduces capacity, which creates further stress.

**Apply it:**
- Reduce demands during bereavement, illness, caregiving crises, or major transitions.
- Avoid assigning recovery work on top of an already full workload.
- Build backup coverage before emergencies occur.

### 5. Self-Determination Theory
**Key figures:** Edward Deci and Richard Ryan.

**Core idea:** Motivation and well-being are supported by autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

**Apply it:**
- Offer choices within clear constraints.
- Provide learning time, constructive feedback, and achievable challenges.
- Build connection without requiring after-hours social participation.

**Watch out:** Autonomy without resources or clear expectations can become abandonment.

### 6. Burnout research
**Key figures:** Christina Maslach and Michael Leiter.

**Core idea:** Burnout involves exhaustion, cynicism or mental distance, and reduced professional efficacy. Their work highlights mismatches in workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values.

**Apply it:**
- Assess all six areas rather than only hours worked.
- Investigate ethical conflict, unfair treatment, and dysfunctional collaboration.
- Respond with organizational changes, alongside access to individual support.

**Watch out:** Burnout is an occupational phenomenon, not a label managers should use to diagnose employees.

## 2. Boundary and recovery theories: protect life outside work

### 7. Boundary theory and work–family border theory
**Key figures:** Christena Nippert-Eng; Blake Ashforth, Glen Kreiner and Mel Fugate; Sue Campbell Clark.

**Core idea:** People differ in how much they prefer to separate or integrate work and personal life. Problems arise when actual boundaries do not match their preferences or obligations.

**Apply it:**
- Ask about preferred availability and communication boundaries.
- Establish shared collaboration windows while protecting off-hours.
- Allow different working patterns without assuming one is more committed.

**Watch out:** “Flexible work” is not healthy if it means permanent availability.

### 8. Work–family conflict
**Key figures:** Jeffrey Greenhaus and Nicholas Beutell.

**Core idea:** Work and family roles can conflict through time demands, strain, or incompatible behavioral expectations.

**Apply it:**
- Use predictable schedules and adequate notice for changes.
- Avoid routine evening meetings.
- Offer leave and coverage that employees can actually use.

**Broaden the lens:** Personal life includes more than parenting—friendships, community, disability management, education, and rest also matter.

### 9. Work–family enrichment
**Key figures:** Jeffrey Greenhaus and Gary Powell.

**Core idea:** Work and personal life can also improve each other through skills, positive emotions, confidence, and support.

**Apply it:**
- Support learning and interests beyond the employee’s immediate role.
- Recognize transferable skills from caregiving, volunteering, or community leadership.
- Create work that leaves people with energy and confidence, not only a paycheck.

**Watch out:** Employees do not need to demonstrate a business benefit to justify a personal life.

### 10. Spillover and crossover
**Key figures:** Mina Westman and researchers in work–family studies.

**Core idea:** Stress can spill from work into an individual’s home life and cross over to partners or colleagues.

**Apply it:**
- Address hostile behavior, unpredictable emergencies, and chronic urgency.
- Consider the wider effects of scheduling changes.
- Recognize that a manager’s after-hours habits can create pressure throughout a team.

### 11. Effort–Recovery model
**Key figures:** Theo Meijman and Gijsbert Mulder.

**Core idea:** Work effort produces strain. Recovery requires sufficient opportunity for that strain to subside; repeated incomplete recovery can accumulate.

**Apply it:**
- Protect breaks and adequate time between shifts.
- Plan lighter periods after unusually intense work.
- Arrange coverage so vacations do not become remote work with different scenery.

**Watch out:** A quiet weekend does not reliably offset months of overload.

### 12. Recovery experiences
**Key figures:** Sabine Sonnentag and Charlotte Fritz.

**Core idea:** Important recovery experiences include psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery outside work, and control over leisure time.

**Apply it:**
- Make disconnecting genuinely safe.
- Avoid requiring employees to monitor channels while off duty.
- Let people choose how they recover.

**Watch out:** Mandatory wellness activities can consume the very personal time they are supposed to protect.

## 3. Organizational frameworks and practices: make balance possible

### 13. Psychological safety
**Key figure:** Amy Edmondson.

**Core idea:** People need to be able to raise concerns, admit errors, and ask for help without fear of interpersonal punishment.

**Apply it:**
- Make “I cannot take this on without dropping something” an acceptable statement.
- Thank people for surfacing capacity risks early.
- Have leaders acknowledge their own limits.

**Watch out:** Psychological safety is not the absence of accountability. It makes honest accountability more possible.

### 14. Organizational justice
**Key figures:** Jerald Greenberg and Jason Colquitt.

**Core idea:** Well-being depends partly on fairness in outcomes, procedures, interpersonal treatment, and explanations.

**Apply it:**
- Make workload allocation and flexibility decisions transparent.
- Check who repeatedly absorbs emergencies or undesirable shifts.
- Provide a credible way to challenge unfair decisions.

**Watch out:** Equal treatment is not always equitable treatment; accommodations may require different arrangements.

### 15. Person–Environment Fit
**Key figures:** John French, Robert Caplan and Robert Harrison; Amy Kristof-Brown in later fit research.

**Core idea:** Strain can arise when job demands exceed someone’s abilities or when work fails to meet their needs and values.

**Apply it:**
- Discuss role scope, preferred working patterns, accessibility, and development needs.
- Redesign roles where possible instead of expecting universal adaptability.
- Revisit fit as people’s circumstances change.

**Watch out:** Do not turn “fit” into a justification for excluding people who differ from the dominant culture.

### 16. Job crafting
**Key figures:** Amy Wrzesniewski and Jane Dutton.

**Core idea:** Employees can reshape aspects of their tasks, relationships, and understanding of their work to improve fit and meaning.

**Apply it:**
- Allow some task exchanges and adjustments.
- Help employees use strengths without permanently assigning them every related task.
- Give explicit permission and time for small role improvements.

**Watch out:** Job crafting cannot repair understaffing or abusive management by itself.

### 17. Results-Only Work Environment
**Key figures:** Cali Ressler and Jody Thompson.

**Core idea:** Evaluate contribution by results rather than visible attendance or hours at a desk.

**Apply it:**
- Define reasonable deliverables and quality standards.
- Reduce presenteeism and unnecessary monitoring.
- Offer location and schedule flexibility where the work permits it.

**Watch out:** Results-only systems can intensify work if targets are unrealistic. Pair them with capacity limits and reasonable working hours.

### 18. Predictable scheduling and the right to disconnect
**Promoted by:** Labor movements, occupational-health advocates, and various national and organizational policies.

**Core idea:** People need usable control over nonwork time, including knowing when they will work and when they will not be expected to respond.

**Apply it:**
- Publish schedules with meaningful notice.
- Specify response expectations and genuine emergency criteria.
- Use compensated, explicit on-call rotations rather than informal universal availability.

**Watch out:** An email disclaimer is not enough if managers reward immediate replies. Legal requirements also vary by jurisdiction.

### 19. Reduced working time and shorter workweeks
**Promoted by:** Juliet Schor and organizations such as 4 Day Week Global, among others.

**Core idea:** Reducing working time may improve well-being and, in some settings, maintain organizational performance.

**Apply it:**
- Pilot reductions alongside meeting cuts, process simplification, and staffing review.
- Track service quality, overtime, workload intensity, and employee well-being.
- Distinguish reduced hours from compressed hours: four ten-hour days are not a reduction.

**Watch out:** Evidence is promising in some contexts, but pilots often involve self-selected organizations. Outcomes depend on role, staffing, and implementation.

### 20. Healthy workplaces and psychosocial risk management
**Key institutions:** World Health Organization; International Organization for Standardization, particularly ISO 45003.

**Core idea:** Psychological health should be managed through systematic hazard identification and prevention, alongside physical safety.

**Apply it:**
- Assess workload, violence and harassment, role ambiguity, isolation, and poor change management.
- Involve employees in identifying risks and choosing controls.
- Assign owners, deadlines, and follow-up measures.

**Watch out:** A well-being survey is not a prevention program unless it leads to action.

## 4. Philosophies: clarify what work is for

These are useful guides to values, not substitutes for occupational-health evidence.

### 21. Human flourishing and eudaimonia
**Key figure:** Aristotle; developed in many contemporary accounts of flourishing.

**Core idea:** A good life involves more than output or pleasure. It includes meaningful activity, relationships, practical judgment, and participation in community.

**Apply it:**
- Treat work as one part of a good life, not its organizing purpose for everyone.
- Respect aspirations that do not involve promotion.
- Make room for learning, relationships, and contribution beyond employment.

### 22. The capabilities approach
**Key figures:** Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum.

**Core idea:** Well-being concerns people’s real opportunities to live lives they have reason to value—not merely the formal options offered to them.

**Apply it:**
- Ask whether employees can actually use leave or flexibility without penalty.
- Check accessibility and the practical needs of caregivers and shift workers.
- Evaluate policy uptake across roles and groups.

**Key question:** Is this a genuine choice, or only a policy on paper?

### 23. Ethics of care and recognition of unpaid work
**Key figures:** Carol Gilligan, Joan Tronto; related economic perspectives from Nancy Folbre and Marilyn Waring.

**Core idea:** Human interdependence and caregiving deserve recognition. Paid employment relies on substantial unpaid labor.

**Apply it:**
- Make caregiving leave and flexibility available across genders.
- Avoid treating uninterrupted availability as the ideal employee profile.
- Recognize and distribute relational work within the team.

**Watch out:** Care should be supported collectively, not assigned by default to women or particularly accommodating employees.

### 24. Decent work
**Key institution:** International Labour Organization.

**Core idea:** Healthy work requires rights, fair income, security, social protection, and worker voice—not just attractive benefits.

**Apply it:**
- Address pay, safety, discrimination, and insecure scheduling before adding wellness perks.
- Include contractors and lower-paid workers in your assessment.
- Consult worker representatives where applicable.

### 25. Slow productivity and sufficiency
**Key figures:** Cal Newport for *Slow Productivity*; E. F. Schumacher for a broader critique of growth and scale.

**Core idea:** Sustainable, high-quality work can require doing fewer things, allowing a natural pace, and rejecting permanent acceleration.

**Apply it:**
- Limit concurrent priorities.
- Replace “What else can we add?” with “What should we stop?”
- Define “good enough” for routine work and reserve exceptional effort for genuinely important situations.

**Watch out:** This is an influential philosophy, not a universally validated operating model. Employees need organizational permission to reduce work in progress.

### 26. Rest as intrinsically valuable
**Key figures:** Abraham Joshua Heschel in the Sabbath tradition; Tricia Hersey in contemporary advocacy for rest.

**Core idea:** Rest does not need to earn its legitimacy by making people more productive later.

**Apply it:**
- Normalize time that is neither paid work nor self-improvement.
- Avoid framing vacation solely as a performance tool.
- Respect different religious and secular ways of protecting nonwork time.

**Watch out:** Do not impose a spiritual practice or preferred leisure activity on employees.

## A coherent combination for your team

You do not need to adopt all 26. A strong starting combination is:

| Purpose | Framework |
|---|---|
| Define your values | Decent work + capabilities approach |
| Diagnose unhealthy conditions | Job Demands–Resources + organizational justice |
| Protect personal life | Boundary theory + predictable scheduling |
| Ensure recovery | Effort–Recovery + recovery experiences |
| Make problems discussable | Psychological safety |
| Sustain improvement | Participatory psychosocial risk management |

Together, these avoid two common mistakes: making employees individually responsible for structural overload, and assuming everyone wants the same work–life arrangement.

## Turn the ideas into a 90-day plan

### Days 1–30: Listen and establish a baseline
Ask employees, confidentially where possible:
- Can you usually finish your work within agreed hours?
- Can you disconnect without consequences?
- Are priorities clear and achievable?
- Is workload distributed fairly?
- Can you use leave and flexibility in practice?
- Do you feel safe raising capacity concerns?

Review overtime, schedule changes, leave usage, turnover, and workload patterns. Use aggregate data and protect confidentiality, especially in small teams.

### Days 31–60: Change the work
Choose two or three concrete interventions, such as:
- Remove low-value meetings or reporting.
- Limit simultaneous projects.
- Establish response-time and after-hours rules.
- Create absence coverage.
- Improve staffing or reduce commitments where capacity is insufficient.

**For task estimates specifically:** Treat estimates as planning inputs, not individual speed targets. Account separately for meetings, coordination, interruptions, and uncertainty; do not fill every available minute with estimated task work.

### Days 61–90: Check whether conditions improved
Evaluate:
- Sustainable hours and perceived workload.
- Ability to disconnect and take leave.
- Schedule predictability.
- Fairness across roles and employee groups.
- Quality and service outcomes.

Keep, revise, or stop interventions based on what employees experience—not merely whether a policy was published.

**Bottom line:** Build a team where reasonable workloads, fair treatment, genuine choice, and protected recovery are normal operating conditions—not rewards people earn after overworking.
